On building products.
Things I keep coming back to.

A growing list. Observations on what to build, where to spend the energy, what to chase, who to listen to.

What to build, before how

Two stages, two different jobs. Most failed products fail in the first one, and don't realize it.

1. Build the right thing first; then build it right.

Two stages, two completely different jobs:

Stage 1 · what to build
  • Anyone want this?
  • Can people use it?
  • Can we build & run it?
▲ Most products fail here, silently.
Stage 2 · how to build it
  • Ship it well.
▲ But everyone blames the failure on here.

The signs you're still in stage one even though you think you're past it:

  • You're still adjusting the problem to fit what you're building.
  • The people who'd use this haven't seen it yet.
  • You're talking to engineering about how before you're confident about what.

Stage one doesn't feel like progress. No demo, no screenshots, just conversations and a slowly sharpening problem. So we skip it. Then a polished thing ships and turns out to solve a problem no one had.

A test for which stage you're really in: when you describe what you're working on, are you describing the shape of someone's day getting better, or the shape of the product? If the second, you haven't left stage one yet.

The corollary: if you're spending more time in stage two than stage one, that's a signal, not a virtue.

Where to spend the energy

Energy is finite. The question isn't "how much can we build". It's "where does an extra hour move the product the most."

2. "Where do we suck, and where does it matter?"

The opportunity is at the overlap of important and disappointing:

↑ important
Don't break it
The opportunity ✓
Don't bother
Don't bother
← working fine disappointing →

The trap: bugs are loud, importance is quiet. Most teams over-fix what's loud and under-fix what's quiet but matters most.

A move: rank the top 20 user complaints twice: once by frequency, once by what the user actually loses if you don't fix it. The two lists overlap less than anyone expects. The gap is where the real work is hiding.

Opportunity = Importance + (Importance − Satisfaction)

The math version of the same question. If something matters to your user and they're disappointed in the current solution, it's a high-opportunity space. Useful as a sanity check on your gut.

3. Don't only ship features. Don't only ship repairs.

Mile wide, inch deep. When you only chase new things.
Repair-only. How a product goes invisible while you work.

The right mix is roughly 70/30 in one direction or the other, depending on stage. The trap is treating it as identity ("we're a builder team" vs. "we're a craft team") instead of a roadmap call to make every quarter.

What to chase

Some products work because they catch a moment. Most last because they sit on top of something that doesn't change. The first is luck. The second is a choice.

4. Make things people want. Not the other way around.

Two kinds of products: ones that catch demand that already exists, and ones that try to create demand for something new. The first is much, much easier.

If you're constantly explaining why your product matters, that's a tell. The version of the product that wins doesn't need explaining.

A test: hand someone the product cold, with no setup, and watch their first move. If they reach for it, you have the first kind of product. If they ask "what's this for?", you're trying to make people want a thing.

5. Focus on what doesn't change.

Bezos: people don't ask what won't be different in ten years. But that's the better question.

trends, fashions, hot apps → here today, gone tomorrow
current behaviors → change slowly
durable demand: cheap, fast, easy, reliable, status → doesn't change. build here.

If you bet on something that won't change, you only have to be right about execution. If you bet on something that will, you have to be right about the future and execution. Two bets are harder than one.

6. If a product is going to take five years of your life, the only thing that earns those years is whether it'll matter.

Trendy is rarely meaningful. Most flavor-of-the-month builds don't survive their own hype cycle.

A test: if this thing works exactly as you hope, will you be proud of it in ten years? If you can't honestly say yes, you're spending the years on the wrong thing.

Who to listen to

Customers say a lot. Most of it is the symptom, not the disease. The job isn't to gather requests. It's to figure out what the requests are about.

7. What customers ask for is never as interesting as why.

The feature request is the symptom. The why underneath is the actual product.

10%The request
"can you add X?"
90%The why
the situation, the constraint, the hassle they want to escape

Build for the request and you ship a feature that misses. Build for the why and you often ship something different from what was asked, and better.

A move: when someone asks for X, ask "what would you do with X?" Then ask the same question on the answer. Three layers down, you're usually at the actual problem.

8. If you want to charge money, don't optimize for people who try to get everything for free.

People on the free tier forever and people who pay you are not the same group. Their feedback is shaped by what they don't have to commit to. They'll happily ask for a feature they wouldn't pay for, or complain about a price they'd never pay anyway.

This doesn't mean ignore them. They may convert when the product becomes a category default. But until then, their feedback shapes the wrong product.

A move: when pricing or deciding features, weight the feedback by who actually pays. That's the population you're optimizing for.

The industry, honestly

Two observations about the shape of this industry I keep finding useful when sizing up a market, or my own product.

9. The hardest problem in B2C is distribution. The hardest problem in B2B is sales.

Two different jobs that look the same on the surface:

B2C
product → user
↑ distribution (the hard part)
B2B
product → buyer → user
↑ sales (the hard part)

The structural difference is the whole game. In B2B there's a buyer who isn't the user, and bridging product → buyer is the work. In B2C the user decides for themselves, but getting in front of them is the work.

If you're a builder who hates this part, pick the half whose hard part you can stomach. The other half is where most products quietly die.

10. Tech, mostly, does two things.

In B2C, the work is developing new habits in consumers: getting people to do something different than they used to.

In B2B, the work is taking a process that exists in a hundred places and dropping the cost of running it, usually by replacing manual work with software.

Almost everything that's worked is one of those two. If your product isn't doing one of them, ask hard whether you're building something, or just adding noise.

Many of these aren't original to me. They're paraphrased from people like Marty Cagan, Tony Fadell, Anthony Ulwick, Dan Olsen, Jeff Bezos, and Patrick McKenzie, among others I've read over the years. My versions are what stuck.
