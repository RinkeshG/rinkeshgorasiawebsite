Why your feature isn't getting used.
Four problems, in the order you should fix them.

When a feature isn't getting used, there's usually one of four reasons. Figuring out which one is most of the work. Pick wrong and you'll spend a quarter on something that needed a tooltip.

The funnel

Before someone uses a feature, there's a sequence they go through. Something can break at any of four points along the way. Each break is a different problem.

The four, in plain terms

  1. Discovery. They don't know the feature exists. Or they know but it's not what comes to mind first when they need that kind of thing.
  2. Use case. They know it exists, but they don't have a clear sense of when they'd reach for it.
  3. Quality. They tried it once. Either it didn't work as well as what they were already using, or it didn't actually do what they came to your product for.
  4. Missing pieces. You have something. A competitor has more. People haven't seen enough reason to leave the competitor and come over.

How to tell which one

All four show up in customer interviews and surveys if you actually talk to people. Beyond that, each one has its own signal:

  1. Discovery. Users using the feature ÷ users who land on its screen.
  2. Use case. Users using the feature ÷ users who know it exists.
  3. Quality. Satisfaction scores. Support tickets named to that feature.
  4. Missing pieces. Competitors named by users in interviews.

If you're not sure which one you're dealing with, just run through them in order. Whichever one fails first is your answer.

Fixing discovery is usually a few days of work. Some copy, a tooltip, a different place in the nav. Building a missing feature, on the other hand, takes a quarter or longer, and the new feature also needs its own discovery, use case, and quality work after it ships.

If the real issue is at #1 and you go after it as a #4, you've put a quarter into something that wanted three days.

The compound trap

And here's the worse version. If you've got #1, #2, and #3 problems still sitting around, your new #4 feature is going to walk into all of them on day one.

what "just build the feature" actually ships

More code doesn't fix the lower problems. It just gives them more places to show up.

There's also a growth thing

One more reason the order matters. With levels 1, 2, and 3, you're working with people who are already in your product. They're already there. You just need to get them to the feature, or help them figure out it's for them, or make it work better.

With #4, the people who'll use the new thing aren't there yet. So before any of them touch it, you have to spend money getting them in.

Most teams don't think of those as different kinds of work. They are.

Why teams jump to #4 anyway

None of these are bad reasons, the incentives just point that way:

  • Building something new feels like progress. A copy change in a tooltip doesn't show up in a demo.
  • Engineering would rather write code than edit copy.
  • Investors and execs see a roadmap with new features and feel like things are happening.
  • The team was hired to build things. So they build.

What this is supposed to do is slow that reflex down for a few minutes. Long enough to run through #1, #2, and #3 before committing a quarter to #4.

How to use this

Three things that work in practice:

  1. Look at your underused features. Pick three of them in your product. Walk each one through these four levels. You'll find most of them are sitting at #1 or #2.
  2. Reserve at least a third of your roadmap for fixes at the bottom three levels. If everything you ship is new, a lot of it is going to be wasted.
  3. Make the level the first thing on a feature kickoff. Before scoping, before tickets, the PM has to say which of the four it is. And if it's a #4, the PM also has to say what's planned to handle the #1, #2, and #3 work the feature itself is going to need.

The roadmap diagnostic

Look at what you shipped last quarter. Tag every item as 1, 2, 3, or 4. If most of it is level 4, you're in the trap. What you ship is the most honest answer to where your team's spending its time.
