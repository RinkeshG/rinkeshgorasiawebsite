How I work.
0 to shipped.

Not a methodology. Just how a product actually moves from a vague problem to something real — based on doing this twice from scratch.

The process

  1. 01

    Get into the room

    Before any brief, any wireframe, any doc — I try to be physically present where the problem lives. With Savior, that meant months inside hospitals before touching a spec. The gap between how things are supposed to work and how they actually work is almost always where the product lives. You can't see that on a call.

  2. 02

    Find the actual problem

    The brief is usually a symptom. Someone says "we need a dashboard" — okay, but why? What decision are they trying to make faster? I spend a lot of time here before writing anything down. The real problem is almost never the stated problem. It's usually one layer deeper.

  3. 03

    Build the smallest useful thing

    Not an MVP in the buzzword sense. The smallest thing that actually does the job for one real person. Career Leap started as a WhatsApp group. KnowYourPay started as a spreadsheet I shared with a few people. If the thing doesn't work before it's a product, it won't work after.

  4. 04

    Ship early, watch what happens

    I have a bias toward shipping early over debating internally. Real users in 3 days beats a perfectly refined prototype in 3 weeks. The feedback you get from watching someone actually use something is worth more than any survey or interview.

  5. 05

    Know when to kill it

    I shut down Career Leap when the unit economics didn't work. I killed Hypersync before it launched when the third person I showed it to said "I just use Doppler." The willingness to stop is as important as the willingness to start. Sunk cost is the enemy of good judgment.

Principles I actually follow

  • —

    Doing beats planning. A working thing teaches you more than a perfect document. I'll always bias toward making something real, even if it's rough.

  • —

    Small teams are a feature. Career Leap peaked at 3 people. Savior was tiny until it wasn't. Overhead kills speed, and speed is usually the advantage.

  • —

    Distribution is part of the product. How people find it, share it, and explain it to someone else — that's not a marketing problem. That's a product problem.

  • —

    Honest numbers over comfortable ones. I'd rather know that retention is bad at week 2 than celebrate week 1 activation. The uncomfortable metric is always the useful one.

  • —

    Shutting down is a decision, not a failure. Career Leap didn't work at scale. That's data. Acting on it is what kept me from wasting another two years on something that wasn't going to work.

Tools I reach for

Figma

Wireframes, flows, quick prototypes. Mostly low-fidelity until something needs to be handed off.

Notion

PRDs, research notes, everything that needs to be shared and commented on.

Linear

Tracking work when there's a team. For solo projects, a plain text file works fine.

Mixpanel

Retention curves and funnel analysis. I set up event tracking before launch, not after.

Claude

First draft of almost everything written. Also for thinking through edge cases I haven't considered.

WhatsApp

Still the fastest way to get early feedback from real people. Career Leap started here.

Loom

Async walkthroughs. Way faster than writing a long Slack message for anything visual.

Google Sheets

KnowYourPay started as a spreadsheet. Still the fastest way to validate something that processes numbers.

What I don't do

  • ✕ Six-week discovery phases before any output. If I can't form a useful hypothesis in a week of real conversations, the problem isn't well-defined enough to build against.
  • ✕ Pixel-perfect prototypes before validation. I've seen too many teams fall in love with a Figma file. Real users interact with real things differently.
  • ✕ Vanity metrics as north stars. Downloads, impressions, signups — only useful in relation to something that matters. I want to know who came back and why.
  • ✕ Consensus-driven product decisions. Feedback is an input, not a vote. Someone has to make the call and own it. That's the job.
